The PDS
Method Map

Product data science on one page. A question enters at the left and leaves as a decision. Measurement is a foundation, not a phase, because what ships changes the data; theory is a ceiling, not a report, because what you learn has to outlive the test that produced it.

the map

what is on the page

1

What is actually on the page

The map has six labelled objects, one decision point, two kinds of arrow, and two bands. Read left to right and you are following the life of a single question. Read top to bottom and you are moving down a stack: a memory runs across the top, the main path sits in the middle, a support layer sits under it, and a foundation sits under that.

Exploratory analytics

Deep dives, funnels, segmentation, retention, root cause, opportunity sizing. The box states its own question: what is happening?

producesA hypothesis worth testing.

Can you randomize?

The only diamond on the map, so the only true fork. Everything upstream is one path; everything downstream splits on this one answer.

producesA routing decision: yes or no.

Experimentation

You control assignment. A/B, switchback, power, sequential tests, HTE (heterogeneous treatment effects: does the effect differ by segment).

producesAn effect size.

Causal inference

Assignment already happened. Synthetic control, DiD (difference-in-differences), matching, IV (instrumental variables), ITS (interrupted time series).

producesAn effect size.

Statistical modeling

Regression, forecasting, simulation, optimization, ML, uplift models. Sits below the main path, not on it. Two outgoing arrows, no incoming one from the question path.

producesEstimators, and automated decisions.

Decision

Ship, kill, reallocate. Drawn as an ellipse rather than a rectangle: it is a terminal state, not another activity.

producesA change to the product, and new data.

Theory layer

The band across the top, drawn as a band because every box touches it. It has two halves. The knowledge repo is prose: one searchable entry per question, holding the design, the number, the decision and what you would do differently, with the losses written up as carefully as the wins. The prior store is a table: for each metric, the distribution of effects the last hundred tests actually produced. The repo is what a person reads before proposing anything; the store is what the platform reads when it sizes the test and again when it shrinks the readout. Not a stage. A memory, and the only object on the map that gets more valuable the longer the team runs.

producesA prior, a realistic MDE, and a question you do not have to ask again.

The two arrow types, and the container

Solid arrows are the path a question travels. Dashed arrows are supports and feedback. That distinction carries most of the meaning on the page: solid means a question is moving forward through a stage, dashed means something is being supplied to a stage or looping back into it. One more dashed line closes the whole system. It leaves the decision, passes through the theory layer along the top, and re-enters exploratory analytics, labelled what ships changes the data. It goes through the ceiling rather than around it because a decision emits two things at once: a change to the product, and something you now know. The map is a loop, not a pipeline.

order

2

Why the stages sit in this order

Think of a doctor. Symptoms come first, then a diagnosis, then a trial of treatment, then a verdict on whether to keep prescribing it. You cannot skip to the trial without a symptom to explain, and you cannot judge the treatment without having measured the patient the same way before and after. The map is that sequence, and each stage answers exactly one question.

Exploratory analytics asks what is happening. It is description. It tells you that checkout completion fell nine points for mobile users in Germany. It cannot tell you why, and it is not supposed to. Its output is a hypothesis, which is a guess with a shape: if we change X, metric Y moves.

The diamond asks whether you can assign treatment. This is the hinge of the entire map. Randomising means you decide who gets the change, by coin flip. That coin flip is what makes the comparison group honest, because it guarantees the two groups differ only by luck and by the treatment.

Experimentation and causal inference both ask how big the effect is. They emit the same currency, an effect size, which is why both arrows into the decision carry that identical label. One earns it by design, the other by reconstruction.

The decision asks what we do about it. Ship, kill, reallocate. This is the point of the whole map, and the reason it is an ellipse: work that ends anywhere else has not finished.

What breaks when you skip a stage

  • Exploratory analyticsYou test guesses instead of hypotheses, and burn the scarcest resource you have, which is traffic. Sizing the opportunity first is what tells you whether a two percent lift is worth a quarter of engineering time.
  • The diamondThe most expensive mistake available to a PDS: you compare users who chose the feature against users who did not, and you call the difference an effect. It is not. Users who adopt a feature were already different.
  • The causal stageYou are shipping on correlation. Some of those calls will be right, and you will never know which ones.
  • The decisionYou have produced a document, not an outcome. The analysis existed to change what the team does.
  • The theory layerEvery quarter starts from zero. You re-run a test somebody ran two years ago, argue from memory about what a banner is worth, and power each experiment as though you had never seen an effect size at this company before. The cost is not one wasted test, it is that nothing compounds.
  • The measurement frameworkEverything above it degrades. If the event that fires on purchase is unreliable, your funnel is wrong, your experiment readout is wrong, and your forecast is trained on noise, all at once and all without warning.

Why measurement is a foundation, not a phase

If measurement were a phase, you would do it once and move on. But the dashed loop from the decision back into exploratory analytics means every ship alters the very data the next question will be asked of. A shipped feature changes user mix, changes which events fire, changes what your baseline means. So the definition of success has to be maintained continuously, like calibrating a scale you weigh things on every day, not like a setup step you did in January. A cracked foundation does not announce itself; the house just leans.

Why theory is a ceiling, not a report

A readout answers one question and stops. What survives it is smaller and more useful: an effect size you can reuse as an expectation, a lever you now know this product responds to, a question you can refuse to reopen. That residue is what the theory layer holds, and it is written in the same motion that closes the test, not in a quarterly write-up nobody reads. Its failure mode is the mirror of the floor’s: a wrong metric returns a confident number, and a stale belief returns a confident answer to a question nobody re-asked. So every entry carries a date, the conditions it held under, and the event that would expire it. The dashed loop is what expires them: the ship that changed the data changed the world the belief was true in.

the service layer

3

Where statistical modeling fits, and why it is off the path

This is the part of the map that is easiest to misread, and the geometry is the answer. Statistical modeling has no incoming solid arrow. No question ever travels into it. It is not a fifth stage of the journey. It sits below the main path with two arrows going out, which makes it a service layer: something the other boxes call on, rather than something a question passes through.

1 · Supplies estimators (dashed)

The dashed line runs up into the causal study design container. Dashed means supports. Modeling is the machinery inside a causal study, not a rival to it. Difference-in-differences is a regression. Synthetic control is a weighted model of a counterfactual. The causal design decides what comparison is valid; modeling is how the number gets computed.

producesThe analysis a trial design calls for.

2 · Automates the decision (solid)

The solid line runs straight to the decision ellipse, bypassing the causal box entirely. This is modeling in production: a forecast that sets inventory, an optimizer that allocates budget, an uplift model that picks who gets the offer. The decision gets made repeatedly, per user or per day, by a system rather than by a person reading a readout.

producesA decision made at volume.

An analogy. The causal boxes are a courtroom: the design of the trial is what makes the verdict trustworthy, and its output is a single judgement about one defendant. Statistical modeling is the forensics lab. In job one it works for the court, running the analysis that the trial design calls for. In job two it skips the court entirely and runs an automated screening system that flags cases by itself, at volume, with no trial at all.

What it needs. Historical data from the measurement framework, which is why an arrow from the band feeds it like every other box. Modeling needs volume and history in a way exploratory analytics does not.

What it gives back. A fitted relationship. A prediction, an estimate with uncertainty around it, a ranking, or an allocation. Note what is missing: on its own it does not output an effect size. Effect sizes come from the causal boxes, which is where both of those labelled arrows originate.

What the neighbours cannot do. Exploratory analytics describes what already happened, one slice at a time. Modeling generalises: it fits a relationship across many variables at once and extends it to cases you have not seen, including the future. That is the thing neither a funnel nor a cohort chart can do.

And what it cannot do. The causal boxes answer did this change cause that. Modeling answers what will happen and which option is best. Those sound similar and are not. A model that predicts churn beautifully tells you nothing about whether your retention campaign reduces churn, because the model learned patterns in a world where the campaign was targeted at the people most likely to churn. Prediction rides on correlation and does not care why. Causal work exists precisely because you are about to intervene, and intervening breaks the correlations the model relied on.

When statistical modeling is the wrong tool

When the question is causal and you reach for a model instead of a design. Adding control variables to a regression does not substitute for randomisation or for a valid comparison group.
When you have not done the exploration yet. Fitting a model to a question you have not described is how you get a technically impressive answer to the wrong question.
When a decision is made once. A model earns its keep on decisions made repeatedly and at volume. A one-time ship-or-kill call needs an effect size, not a forecast.
When the measurement framework is shaky. Modeling amplifies instrumentation problems rather than revealing them, because a model will happily fit noise and report confidence.

inside the box

4

What is inside the experimentation box

The map draws experimentation as one rectangle, which is honest at two tests a quarter and misleading at two hundred. The rectangle contains six steps, and five of them have a way of handing back a confident wrong number without the readout looking any different. An experimentation platform is the thing that makes those five steps configuration instead of craft, and the reason mature teams describe themselves as running a learning system rather than running tests.

Where each step breaks

  • Idea“Let us test blue” is not a hypothesis. Problem, mechanism, metric, prior: if you cannot write the mechanism, there is nothing for the test to confirm or falsify, and whatever comes back you will explain after the fact. No mechanism, no test, is a cheap rule that removes most of the bad ones.
  • DesignThe prior store says what this metric has actually done across the last hundred tests, so the MDE stops being a wish. Effects are small and most ideas do nothing: seventy to ninety percent of experiments are killed or neutral everywhere it has been measured, which means the honest prior mean is roughly zero. A test powered for a lift nobody at this company has ever produced was decided before it launched.
  • AssignmentFlags, a randomisation service, layers so concurrent tests do not interact, and holdouts. Then the case a bigger sample cannot fix: when treating one user changes another user’s experience, through shared supply, a feed, or an inventory both sides draw from, your control group is treated through the back door. That is switchback or cluster randomisation, decided at design time or not at all.
  • LoggingExposure, not eligibility. If the log records who was bucketed rather than who actually saw the variant, the estimate is diluted toward zero and nothing downstream flags it, because the test looks exactly like a test that ran. This is the most common bug in the chain and the one that survives longest.
  • AnalysisCUPED against pre-period covariates cuts the sample you need, often by half. The SRM check asks whether randomisation actually held. Sequential or always-valid p-values make daily peeking a licensed thing to do rather than a violation, which matters because the platform will show the PM a curve on day three whatever you agreed. ATE says whether it worked, CATE says who it worked for, and empirical Bayes shrinkage pulls the estimate back toward what this metric usually does, which is the correction for the winner’s curse.
  • DecisionShip, kill, or roll back, then write it up either way. A rollback triggered by a guardrail is the platform working, not a failure. The only real failure is a finished test whose result goes nowhere, because that is the loop staying open.

What an experimentation platform actually is

Assignment, plus logging, plus a causal engine, plus memory. Most companies build the first two, call the result a platform, and cannot work out why the wins never add up to anything. The engine is where the sample gets halved and the false positives get caught. The memory is where the next test gets sized and the last one stops being repeated. On a mature team you file an experiment the way you file a pull request: the metric comes from a registry rather than a text box, the system fills in the detectable effect from the prior distribution for that metric, guardrails attach themselves, and randomisation, the ratio check, variance reduction and the sequential test are defaults you would have to work to switch off. Nothing reaches every user without a link back to the experiment that justified it.

Measure learning, not wins

Win rates are low everywhere and a team judged on them will stop proposing anything that could fail. Spotify has published its own numbers against exactly that incentive: roughly one experiment in eight wins, while around two thirds teach the team enough to make a decision, and the four in ten stopped by a guardrail count as the system doing its job. The metric that survives is whether the experiment produced a decision, not whether it produced a lift, and it is the only version of the count that rewards testing the risky idea.

day to day

5

Using the map day to day

Turn the map into three questions you ask in order, before you open a query editor, with one lookup above them and one check underneath. That bracket is the map’s own shape. It takes about a minute and it is the whole discipline.

  • Before all threeHas this been answered, and what does this metric usually do? Search the theory layer before you scope anything. Three outcomes: it is settled and you hand back the entry, it is stale and you have a prior instead of a blank page, or it is genuinely new. Either way the prior store gives you the effect size this metric has historically moved by, which is what turns the MDE from a wish into a number. The lookup costs a minute and the test costs a sprint.
  • Description or change?If the sentence contains what, where, how many, who, you are in exploratory analytics. If it contains because, caused, lift, impact, worth it, you are heading for the diamond.
  • Who assigned treatment?If you can assign it, or could have, go into experimentation. If assignment already happened, by launch date, by geography, by self-selection, go into causal inference and pick the method that matches how assignment happened. This is the single highest-leverage habit on the map.
  • Once, or continuously?Once means you need an effect size. Continuously, per user or per day, means you are building for the statistical modeling box and the solid arrow into the decision.
  • And underneath all threeDo I trust the metric? If the answer is no, the measurement framework is the actual work, and everything above it is premature.

Worked routings

The question as it arrivesRoutes toWhy, and what you get back
"Mobile checkout conversion dropped four points last month. What happened?"Exploratory analyticsPure description, no intervention proposed. Funnel and segmentation work until the drop localises to a step, a platform, a cohort. Output is a hypothesis, which then enters the diamond.
"Does the new one-page checkout increase completion?"ExperimentationYou control who sees which checkout, so randomise. Power the test before you launch it, not after. Output is an effect size with a confidence interval, and the decision is ship or kill.
"We rolled the new pricing page out to all users in March. Did it help?"Causal inferenceAssignment already happened and it was not random; everyone got it at once. That is an interrupted time series, or DiD against a comparable unaffected market. Output is still an effect size, with assumptions you must state.
"Users who enable notifications retain better. Should we push everyone to enable them?"ExperimentationThe trap case. The observed gap is self-selection, not effect: engaged users enable notifications. Randomise the prompt, not the outcome. If you truly cannot, matching or IV in the causal inference box, with the bias acknowledged.
"Which of our twelve million users should get the win-back discount this week?"Statistical modelingA decision made repeatedly at volume, so it belongs to the automation arrow. An uplift model ranks who is moved by the discount, not who is likely to return anyway. Validate the model itself with an experiment.
"Does the new courier bonus increase deliveries completed?"Experimentation, redesignedRandomising couriers is the obvious move and it is wrong: pay one courier more and they take the jobs a control courier would have taken, so the control group is treated through the back door and the effect looks larger than it is. Randomise time or city rather than people. Switchback or cluster, decided before launch, because no sample size fixes interference.
"Didn't we try something like this two years ago?"Theory layerA lookup, not an analysis. The entry says what was tested, what the effect was, and what has changed since. If it predates the redesign it is a prior rather than an answer: it sharpens the next test, sets a realistic expectation, and stops the team arguing from memory.
"Our activation number differs in two dashboards and nobody trusts either."Measurement frameworkNot a stage on the path, a foundation crack. Fix the definition of success and the source of truth first; every analysis built on top of it inherits the error invisibly.

Four habits worth stealing

Name the box out loud in the kickoff. Saying "this is a causal inference question, not an experiment, because the rollout already happened" resets stakeholder expectations about certainty before you produce a number, not after.

Treat the diamond as a gate, not a formality. Most bad product analytics is a diamond that was never asked. When someone shows you a difference between two groups, ask who put them in those groups.

Close the loop. The dashed return line means the job is not over at ship. What you shipped changed the data your next question will be asked of, so go back and check that the metric still means what it meant.

Write the belief, not just the result. "Variant B won, +2.1% on checkout completion" is a readout and it dies with the ticket. "This product responds to friction removal at the payment step, about two points, and it held for six weeks" is a belief, and the next three questions start from it. One sentence, written while the test is still open, into the knowledge base rather than the tracker.

compressed

6

Holding the whole thing in your head

Compressed to something you can carry without looking at it, the map is three method buckets, a floor underneath them, a memory above them, and a layer of judgment on top of that. The shape carries as much meaning as the contents. Measurement is not a fourth method, theory is not a fifth, and routing is not a method at all.

Routing & judgment · the layer on top

Which bucket this question belongs in, what the answer would cost, and how much confidence it can carry.

Not on the map, because the map draws methods and this is the judgment about which method a question deserves.

Theory · the ceiling

What we now believe about this product: which levers moved it, by how much, for whom, and which questions are settled.

Above the buckets rather than beside them, because it is not a way of answering a question. It is what the answers accumulate into, and what the next question starts from. Its key is empty: it is not a method.

Measurement · the floor

Are we counting the right thing, the same way, everywhere: definitions, instrumentation, source of truth.

Underneath rather than beside, because you do not choose it against the others. You stand on it.

The test for any bucket scheme. Each bucket needs its own question, its own output, and its own failure mode. If two candidates share all three, they are one bucket wearing two hats. Run the test on experimentation and causal inference: same question, did this cause that; same output, an effect size; same failure mode, an invalid comparison group. Different circumstances, one bucket. That is precisely what the dashed container on the map is asserting, and it is why the count is three and not four.

Measurement sits underneath rather than beside because you never choose it against the others. Its failure mode is also the only one invisible from the inside: a wrong definition does not throw an error, it returns a confident number.

The comparison group breaks in more ways than the diamond suggests. Randomising is necessary and not sufficient, which is why the causation bucket has one failure mode and an experimentation platform has four checks for it. Assignment can drift, so the sample ratio gets tested. Exposure can go unlogged, so half the treated group is counted as untreated and the effect is pulled toward zero. Units can interfere, so the control is treated through a shared resource. Peeking can turn a null into a winner, so the test is sequential from the start. All four produce the same symptom as self-selection, a comparison that is not a comparison, and the readout looks identical either way.

The theory layer fails the bucket test on purpose. It has no question of its own and it produces no estimate, so it is not a fourth bucket. What it has is a failure mode none of the others has: it goes stale without anyone noticing, because a belief that was true when the product was different still reads as true. That is why entries are dated and why the ship that contradicts one is the event that expires it. Buckets answer questions; the ceiling is where the answers are kept so the next question is smaller.

Routing does not appear on the map at all, because the map draws methods and routing is the judgment about which method a question deserves. Executing well inside a bucket is table stakes. Recognising that the question in front of you cannot be answered by the bucket everyone assumed, and saying so before the work starts, is the part that compounds.

Back to the doctor one last time. The three buckets are the instruments. Measurement is whether the instruments are calibrated. Routing is the diagnosis, and nobody values a radiologist for operating the machine.

Three buckets, a floor, a ceiling, and a layer of judgment. The vertical order is the claim.
