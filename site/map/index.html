The method map

Product data science on one page. A question enters at the left and leaves as a decision. Measurement is a foundation, not a phase, because what ships changes the data; theory is a ceiling, not a report, because what you learn has to outlive the test that produced it.

The eight objects, and what each one produces

The map has six labelled objects, one decision point, two kinds of arrow, and two bands. Read left to right and you are following the life of a single question. Read top to bottom and you are moving down a stack: a memory runs across the top, the main path sits in the middle, a support layer sits under it, and a foundation sits under that.

  1. 1

    Exploratory analytics

    Deep dives, funnels, segmentation, retention, root cause, opportunity sizing. The box states its own question: what is happening?

    producesA hypothesis worth testing.

  2. 2

    Can you randomise?

    The only diamond on the map, so the only true fork. Everything upstream is one path; everything downstream splits on this answer.

    producesA routing decision: yes or no.

  3. 3

    Experimentation

    You control assignment. A/B, switchback, power, sequential tests, HTE.

    producesAn effect size.

  4. 4

    Causal inference

    Assignment already happened. Synthetic control, DiD, matching, IV, ITS.

    producesAn effect size.

  5. 5

    Statistical modeling

    Forecasts, rankings, allocations, and the estimators the causal designs run on: below the main path, not on it.

    producesEstimators, and automated decisions.

  6. 6

    Decision

    Ship, kill, reallocate. An ellipse because it is a terminal state, not another activity.

    producesA change to the product, and new data.

  7. 7

    Theory layer

    Two halves. The knowledge repo is prose: one entry per question, holding the design, the number, the decision and what you would do differently. The prior store is a table: for each metric, the distribution of effects the last hundred tests produced. Not a stage; a memory, and the only object on the map that gets more valuable the longer a team runs it.

    producesA prior, a realistic MDE, and a question you do not have to ask again.

  8. 8

    Measurement framework

    The band underneath everything: north-star and core metrics, event instrumentation, the source of truth, the definition of success.

    producesA number the rest of the map can stand on.

3 · order

Why the stages sit in this order

Think of a doctor. Symptoms come first, then a diagnosis, then a trial of treatment, then a verdict on whether to keep prescribing it. You cannot skip to the trial without a symptom to explain, and you cannot judge the treatment without having measured the patient the same way before and after. The map is that sequence, and each stage answers exactly one question.

Each stage answers one question

Exploratory analytics asks what is happening. It is description. It tells you that checkout completion fell nine points for mobile users in Germany. It cannot tell you why, and it is not supposed to. Its output is a hypothesis, which is a guess with a shape: if we change X, metric Y moves.

The diamond asks whether you can assign treatment. This is the hinge of the entire map. Randomising means you decide who gets the change, by coin flip. That coin flip is what makes the comparison group honest, because it guarantees the two groups differ only by luck and by the treatment.

Experimentation and causal inference both ask how big the effect is. They emit the same currency, an effect size, which is why both arrows into the decision carry that identical label. One earns it by design, the other by reconstruction.

The decision asks what we do about it. Ship, kill, reallocate. This is the point of the whole map, and the reason it is an ellipse: work that ends anywhere else has not finished.

What breaks when you skip a stage

  • Exploratory analyticsYou test guesses instead of hypotheses, and burn the scarcest resource you have, which is traffic. Sizing the opportunity first is what tells you whether a two percent lift is worth a quarter of engineering time.
  • The diamondThe most expensive mistake available to a PDS: you compare users who chose the feature against users who did not, and you call the difference an effect. It is not. Users who adopt a feature were already different.
  • The causal stageYou are shipping on correlation. Some of those calls will be right, and you will never know which ones.
  • The decisionYou have produced a document, not an outcome. The analysis existed to change what the team does.
  • The theory layerEvery quarter starts from zero. You re-run a test somebody ran two years ago, argue from memory about what a banner is worth, and power each experiment as though you had never seen an effect size at this company before. The cost is not one wasted test, it is that nothing compounds.
  • The measurement frameworkEverything above it degrades. If the event that fires on purchase is unreliable, your funnel is wrong, your experiment readout is wrong, and your forecast is trained on noise, all at once and all without warning.

4 · the service layer

Where statistical modeling fits, and why it is off the path

Statistical modeling has no incoming solid arrow. No question ever travels into it, so it is not a fifth stage of the journey. It sits below the main path with two arrows going out, which makes it a service layer: something the other boxes call on, rather than something a question passes through.

The two arrows out

1 · Supplies estimators (dashed)

The dashed line runs up into the causal study design container. Dashed means supports. Modeling is the machinery inside a causal study, not a rival to it. Difference-in-differences is a regression. Synthetic control is a weighted model of a counterfactual. The causal design decides what comparison is valid; modeling is how the number gets computed.

producesThe analysis a trial design calls for.

2 · Automates the decision (solid)

The solid line runs straight to the decision ellipse, bypassing the causal box entirely. This is modeling in production: a forecast that sets inventory, an optimizer that allocates budget, an uplift model that picks who gets the offer. The decision gets made repeatedly, per user or per day, by a system rather than by a person reading a readout.

producesA decision made at volume.

What modeling needs, gives, and cannot do

What it needs. Historical data from the measurement framework, which is why an arrow from the band feeds it like every other box. Modeling needs volume and history in a way exploratory analytics does not.

What the neighbours cannot do. Exploratory analytics describes what already happened, one slice at a time. Modeling generalises: it fits a relationship across many variables at once and extends it to cases you have not seen, including the future. That is the thing neither a funnel nor a cohort chart can do.

What it gives back, and what it does not. A fitted relationship: a prediction, an estimate with uncertainty around it, a ranking, or an allocation. Not an effect size. The causal boxes answer did this change cause that; modeling answers what will happen and which option is best. A model that predicts churn beautifully tells you nothing about whether your retention campaign reduces churn, because it learned patterns in a world where the campaign was aimed at the people most likely to churn. Prediction rides on correlation and does not care why. Causal work exists precisely because you are about to intervene, and intervening breaks the correlations the model relied on.

When statistical modeling is the wrong tool

When the question is causal and you reach for a model instead of a design. Adding control variables to a regression does not substitute for randomisation or for a valid comparison group.
When you have not done the exploration yet. Fitting a model to a question you have not described is how you get a technically impressive answer to the wrong question.
When a decision is made once. A model earns its keep on decisions made repeatedly and at volume. A one-time ship-or-kill call needs an effect size, not a forecast.
When the measurement framework is shaky. Modeling amplifies instrumentation problems rather than revealing them, because a model will happily fit noise and report confidence.

5 · inside the box

What is inside the experimentation box

The map draws experimentation as one rectangle, which is honest at two tests a quarter and misleading at two hundred. The rectangle contains six steps, and five of them have a way of handing back a wrong number without the readout looking any different. An experimentation platform is the thing that makes those five steps configuration instead of craft, and the reason mature teams describe themselves as running a learning system rather than running tests.

Where each step breaks

Each of the six steps has its own way of handing back a wrong number, and the corrections are the skills, not the map. designing-experiments covers the four choices that cannot be repaired after launch, with the MDE taken from the prior store. reading-experiments covers whether the result is a result: SRM, exposure, the sequential bound, CUPED, shrinkage.

6 · day to day

Using the map day to day

Search the theory layer before you scope anything. Either the question is settled and you hand back the entry, or the prior store gives you the effect size this metric has historically moved by, which is what turns the MDE from a wish into a number. The lookup costs a minute and the test costs a sprint. Then route the question, and underneath all of it ask whether you trust the metric at all: if you do not, the measurement framework is the actual work.

Worked routings

The question as it arrivesRoutes toWhy, and what you get back
"Mobile checkout conversion dropped four points last month. What happened?"Exploratory analyticsPure description, no intervention proposed. Funnel and segmentation work until the drop localises to a step, a platform, a cohort. Output is a hypothesis, which then enters the diamond.
"Does the new one-page checkout increase completion?"ExperimentationYou control who sees which checkout, so randomise. Power the test before you launch it, not after. Output is an effect size with a confidence interval, and the decision is ship or kill.
"We rolled the new pricing page out to all users in March. Did it help?"Causal inferenceAssignment already happened and it was not random; everyone got it at once. That is an interrupted time series, or DiD against a comparable unaffected market. Output is still an effect size, with assumptions you must state.
"Users who enable notifications retain better. Should we push everyone to enable them?"ExperimentationThe trap case. The observed gap is self-selection, not effect: engaged users enable notifications. Randomise the prompt, not the outcome. If you truly cannot, matching or IV in the causal inference box, with the bias acknowledged.
"Which of our twelve million users should get the win-back discount this week?"Statistical modelingA decision made repeatedly at volume, so it belongs to the automation arrow. An uplift model ranks who is moved by the discount, not who is likely to return anyway. Validate the model itself with an experiment.
"Does the new courier bonus increase deliveries completed?"Experimentation, redesignedRandomising couriers is the obvious move and it is wrong: pay one courier more and they take the jobs a control courier would have taken, so the control group is treated through the back door and the effect looks larger than it is. Randomise time or city rather than people. Switchback or cluster, decided before launch, because no sample size fixes interference.
"Didn't we try something like this two years ago?"Theory layerA lookup, not an analysis. The entry says what was tested, what the effect was, and what has changed since. If it predates the redesign it is a prior rather than an answer: it sharpens the next test, sets a realistic expectation, and stops the team arguing from memory.
"Our activation number differs in two dashboards and nobody trusts either."Measurement frameworkNot a stage on the path, a foundation crack. Fix the definition of success and the source of truth first; every analysis built on top of it inherits the error invisibly.
